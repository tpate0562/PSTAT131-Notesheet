1. A KNN classifier has excellent performance on the training data but noticeably worse 
performance on new data. Which change would be most likely to help? 
a. Decrease k 
b. Increase k 
c. Give variables with larger variances more weight 
d. Include an interaction term 
Solution: A KNN model that is overfitting is likely because the value of k is small. 
Increasing k will likely improve performance. Decreasing k will only make the problem 
worse. Weighting variables with larger variances more heavily will worsen performance. 
Including an interaction term is not necessary (KNN is nonparametric).  
2. A student says, "The most flexible model is always preferable because it can represent 
more complicated relationships." Explain what is wrong with this reasoning. 
Solution: This is not true. There are many cases where the most flexible model is not 
preferable, either because the more flexible model is less interpretable and the goal is 
interpretation, or because the more flexible model can fit too closely to patterns in the 
training data that do not exist in the population, leading to overfitting (low training error 
and high testing error).

4. A student makes these three claims: 
a. A predictor with zero Pearson correlation with the outcome cannot improve 
prediction. 
b. Two highly correlated predictors necessarily interact. 
c. A model with lower training error is always preferable. 
For each claim, state whether it is true or false and explain. 
Solution: For (a), this is false. Predictors with zero Pearson correlation with the outcome do 
not have a linear correlation with the outcome but may very well be able to improve 
predictions in nonlinear and/or empirically driven models. For (b), this is also false. 
Interactions have nothing to do with whether predictors are correlated. An interaction 
means the relationship between X1 and Y differs depending on the value of X2. For (c), this 
is also false. Training error is not a good estimator of testing error. As training error goes to 
zero, testing error almost always increases.

6. A student standardizes age but forgets to standardize annual income before fitting KNN. 
Explain why this is a problem even though both variables are numeric. 
Solution: This is a problem because KNN is a distance-based algorithm. Income most likely 
has a much larger variance than age, and therefore will have a much larger impact on 
distance calculations if it is not also standardized. 
7. A researcher wants to use KNN to predict whether or not a bank customer will default on 
a loan. They write the following code:

  recipe(defaulted ~ ., data = loans) |> 
  step_dummy(all_nominal_predictors(), one_hot = TRUE)

matters and how you would correct it. 
 
Solution: Here are all problems with this analysis, along with why they matter and 
how to correct: 
- 
“.” syntax will include all variables, including application_id, which is an

8. A scatterplot of X and Y shows a very strong U-shaped relationship. Their Pearson 
correlation is 0.03. Which statement is best? 
a. X and Y are essentially unrelated 
b. The scatterplot is likely wrong 
c. There is little linear correlation, despite a strong nonlinear relationship 
d. X will not be a useful variable for predicting Y 
e. Both C and D 
Solution: The predictors have a strong U-shaped (nonlinear) relationship, but little linear 
correlation. This does not mean X will not be useful for predicting Y; nor does it mean they 
are unrelated. It also does not mean the scatterplot is wrong. 
9. Which EDA finding is evidence of an interaction? 
a. X1 and X2 have a Pearson correlation of 0.91 
b. The relationship between X1 and Y is positive for one level of X2 and 
negative for the other 
c. X1 is strongly right-skewed and X2 is strongly left-skewed 
d. X1 and Y have a Pearson correlation of 0.80 
e. The relationship between X1 and Y is the same across both levels of X2 
Solution: B is the definition of an interaction. Pearson correlation does not imply the 
existence of an interaction. Neither does skew.

12.  A student creates a new variable, CompletionRate. CompletionRate is calculated as the 
ratio of CreditsCompleted to CreditsAttempted. Would including CompletionRate as a 
predictor constitute target leakage? Explain. 
Solution: No. CompletionRate is just the proportion of credits that a student attempted in 
their first year that they were actually able to complete, and it is not leakage (since the 
outcome is graduation at the end of four years). One could argue, however, that 
CompletionRate, CreditsCompleted, and CreditsAttempted should not all be included as 
predictors simultaneously (though that is not the question being asked). 
13. A student wants to predict “salary” using “experience” and “department.” They fit the 
following model: 
salary_recipe <- recipe(salary ~ experience + department, 
                        data = employees) %>% 
  step_dummy(department, one_hot = TRUE) 
 
salary_model <- linear_reg() %>% 
  set_engine("lm") 
 
salary_workflow <- workflow() %>% 
  add_recipe(salary_recipe) %>% 
  add_model(salary_model) 
 
salary_fit <- fit(salary_workflow, data = employees) 
 
Suppose “department” has three levels, “Sales,” “IT,” and “HR.” 
 
After fitting the model, R reports that the model matrix is rank-deficient and that one or 
more coefficients cannot be uniquely identified. 
a. What caused the problem? 
Solution: One-hot encoding; this creates three indicator variables, one per level of 
“department,” which results in a linear dependency that OLS regression cannot handle. 
b. Why does “one_hot = TRUE” create this problem for an ordinary linear 
regression model with an intercept? 
Solution: Because the model contains an intercept, which is itself a column of ones. 
Therefore, the design matrix is not full rank. 
c. How should the code be changed? 
Solution: Set “one_hot = FALSE.” 
d. After making this change, how many indicator variables will be created for 
“department”? 
Solution: Two. 
14. A logistic regression model predicts whether a patient has pneumonia based on symptoms 
and clinical measurements. For one patient, the model produces a predicted probability of 
0.78. Which interpretation is correct? 
a. The model is 78% accurate for this patient. 
b. The patient definitely has pneumonia. 
c. The fitted model assigns this patient a probability of 0.78 of belonging to the 
pneumonia class. 
d. 78% of the patient's symptoms indicate pneumonia. 
Solution: The model cannot be “78% accurate” for an individual patient. Nor does this 
predicted probability tell us anything about the patient’s symptoms. We cannot be 
absolutely certain the patient either does or doesn’t have pneumonia. C is the correct 
definition.

16. A logistic regression predicts whether a customer will cancel a subscription. The 
coefficient for the predictor MonthlyFee is 0.04. 
 
A student writes: “A $1 increase in the monthly fee causes a customer’s probability of 
canceling to increase by 4%.” 
 
Identify one problem with this interpretation and provide a better interpretation. 
 
Solution: This statement implies that there is a linear relationship between monthly 
fee and probability of cancellation. There is not. There is a linear relationship 
between monthly fee and log odds of cancellation, or logit. Also, we cannot assume 
that we can draw causal conclusions. A better interpretation would be either “A $1 
increase in the monthly fee is associated with a 0.04 increase in log odds,” or “An 
increase in the monthly fee is associated with an increased probability of 
cancellation” (this is known because the sign of the coefficient is positive).

18. A student says: “I changed my classification threshold from 0.50 to 0.30, so the ROC 
AUC of my fitted model will change.” Is this correct? Explain. 
Solution: This is not correct. The ROC AUC measures the area under the ROC curve, 
which is a curve that plots true positive rates and false positive rates across thresholds. 
Therefore, using a different threshold value to classify observations will have no impact 
whatsoever on the area under the ROC curve.  
19. Suppose the ROC curve below represents a model used to screen patients for a very 
dangerous disease. A false negative is considered substantially worse than a false 
positive. Of points A, B, and C, which threshold would you be most inclined to choose? 
 
a. Point A 
b. Point B 
c. Point C 
d. All points are equally good 
e. Not enough information to decide 
Solution: The problem implies that we are only interested in maximizing our true positive 
rate (and therefore, minimizing our false negative rate), regardless of our false positive 
rate. Therefore, we would prefer the threshold corresponding to Point C (despite a false 
positive rate of almost 50%). 
20. You have 50,000 observations, two predictors, and clear evidence that the covariance 
structures differ substantially across levels of your outcome variable. Which would you 
expect to have the greater potential to capture the true decision boundary? 
a. LDA 
b. QDA 
c. Both would do equally well 
d. Neither would do well because they are both parametric models 
Solution: LDA assumes that the covariance structure does not differ and therefore would 
not do well. QDA would likely excel despite estimating more predictors; p is not large, and 
there are a huge number of observations (so parametric models are fine).
