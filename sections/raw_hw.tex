\begin{qbox}{HW1 Q5}
\textbf{Prompt.} Predictive models are frequently used in machine learning, and they can usually be described as either \ix{mechanistic model}{s} or \ix{empirically-driven model}{s}. Answer the following questions.
\begin{itemize}
\item Define mechanistic. Define empirically-driven. How do these model types differ? How are they similar?
\item In general, is a mechanistic or empirically-driven model easier to understand? Explain your choice.
\item Describe how the \ix{bias-variance tradeoff} is related to the use of mechanistic or empirically-driven models.
\end{itemize}
\mine \textbf{Define mechanistic. Define empirically-driven. How do these model types differ? How are they similar?}

Mechanistic models\ixh{mechanistic model} explain the relationships in the data given pre-established laws and theory, making the data highly explainable, however the model may be more biased due to the reliance on pre-existing laws as frameworks. Empirically driven models\ixh{empirically-driven model} will be more able to catch complex patterns in the data that a mechanistic model could not, as it is solely reliant on mathematics without needing to be bound by physical laws. It will have a higher variance on unseen or confusing data, where a mechanistic model will be highly predictable even when given unseen data. They both explain the same data, but use different methods to reach a conclusion.

\textbf{In general, is a mechanistic or empirically-driven model easier to understand? Explain your choice.}

Mechanistic models are much easier to explain as they are created from pre-existing relationships and can be made into a logical, cohesive, progressive explanation of the patterns in the data. Empirical models are closer to black boxes that ingest much more complex relationships and are not as intuitive to understand\ixh{interpretability}

\textbf{Describe how the bias-variance tradeoff is related to the use of mechanistic or empirically-driven models.}

Mechanistic models are more biased\ixh{bias} since they force the data into an assumed form that may not match the true relationship, however the variance\ixh{variance} is lower on mechanistic models as they are hard-coded in known principles that are less likely to give uncertainty. One model gives lower variance but in return for it you get biases due to assumptions you're bound by, where the other (empirical) is unbiased but can give higher variance on unknown data. If a found pattern is inaccurate when generalized well outside the training range, it could add variance that just wouldn't be found in mechanistic models
\ans (correction, \src{S1.49}) In this course \textbf{mechanistic = \ix{parametric model}}: \emph{assume a parametric form for $f$} (e.g.\ $\beta_0+\beta_1X_1+\cdots$); it won't match the true unknown $f$; adding parameters = more \ix{flexibility}; too many = \ix{overfitting}. \textbf{Empirically-driven = \ix{non-parametric model}}: no/few assumptions about $f$; requires a larger number of observations; much more flexible by default; can also overfit. \emph{Similar}: both estimate $f$ in $Y=f(X_1,\dots,X_p)+\epsilon$ and both can overfit. Empirical models are not ``unbiased'' --- they have \emph{lower} bias and higher variance; mechanistic models have higher bias, lower variance.
\end{qbox}

\textbf{Prompt (Required for 231 Students)} --- \emph{231 only; not answered in my (131) submission, answers below are mine.} In lecture, we presented the general bias-variance tradeoff, which takes the form:
\[ E[(y_0-\hat{f}(x_0))^2]=Var(\hat{f}(x_0))+[Bias(\hat{f}(x_0))]^2+Var(\epsilon) \]
where the underlying model $Y=f(X)+\epsilon$ satisfies the following:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\epsilon$ is a zero-mean random noise term and $X$ is non-random (all randomness in $Y$ comes from $\epsilon$);
\item $(x_0, y_0)$ represents a test observation, independent of the training set, drawn from the same model;
\item $\hat{f}(.)$ is the estimate of $f$ obtained from the training set.
\end{itemize}

\begin{qbox}{HW2 Q10 (231 only)}
\textbf{Prompt.} Which term(s) in the bias-variance tradeoff above represent the \ix{reducible error}? Which term(s) represent the \ix{irreducible error}?
\ans Reducible: $Var(\hat f(x_0)) + [Bias(\hat f(x_0))]^2$ (depend on the method/fit). Irreducible: $Var(\epsilon)$ (noise; no model can remove it).
\end{qbox}

\begin{qbox}{HW2 Q11 (231 only)}
\textbf{Prompt.} Using the bias-variance tradeoff above, demonstrate that the expected test error is always at least as large as the irreducible error.\ixh{testing error}
\ans $Var(\hat f(x_0))\ge 0$ (a variance) and $[Bias(\hat f(x_0))]^2\ge 0$ (a square), so $E[(y_0-\hat f(x_0))^2] = (\text{nonneg.}) + (\text{nonneg.}) + Var(\epsilon) \ge Var(\epsilon)$.
\end{qbox}

\begin{qbox}{HW2 Q12 (231 only)}
\textbf{Prompt.} Prove the bias-variance tradeoff.

Hints:
\begin{itemize}
\item use the definition of $Bias(\hat{f}(x_0))=E[\hat{f}(x_0)]-f(x_0)$;
\item reorganize terms in the expected test error by adding and subtracting $E[\hat{f}(x_0)]$
\end{itemize}
\ans Write $\hat f=\hat f(x_0)$, $f=f(x_0)$, $y_0=f+\epsilon$. Since $\epsilon$ (test noise) is independent of $\hat f$ (training data) and $E[\epsilon]=0$:
\begin{align*}
E[(y_0-\hat f)^2] &= E[(f-\hat f)^2] + 2E[\epsilon]\,E[f-\hat f] + E[\epsilon^2]\\
 &= E[(f-\hat f)^2] + Var(\epsilon).
\end{align*}
Add and subtract $E[\hat f]$ ($f$ and $E[\hat f]$ are constants):
\begin{align*}
E[(f-\hat f)^2] &= E[(f-E\hat f + E\hat f-\hat f)^2]\\
&= (f-E\hat f)^2 + 2(f-E\hat f)\,\underbrace{E[E\hat f-\hat f]}_{=0} + E[(\hat f-E\hat f)^2]\\
&= [Bias(\hat f)]^2 + Var(\hat f).
\end{align*}
Hence $E[(y_0-\hat f(x_0))^2]=Var(\hat f(x_0))+[Bias(\hat f(x_0))]^2+Var(\epsilon)$. $\square$\ixh{bias}\ixh{variance}\ixh{bias-variance tradeoff}
\end{qbox}

\textbf{Prompt (Required for 231 Students)} --- \emph{231 only; concise answers.} In a binary classification problem, let $p$ represent the probability of class label $1$, which implies that $1 - p$ represents the probability of class label $0$. The \emph{\ix{logistic function}} (also called the ``inverse logit'') is the cumulative distribution function of the logistic distribution, which maps a real number \emph{z} to the open interval $(0, 1)$.

\begin{qbox}{HW3 Q11 (231 only)}
\textbf{Prompt.} Given that:
\[ p(z)=\frac{e^z}{1+e^z} \]
Prove that the inverse of a logistic function is indeed the \emph{\ix{logit}} function:
\[ z(p)=ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) \]
\ans $p(1+e^z)=e^z \Rightarrow p = e^z(1-p) \Rightarrow e^z=\dfrac{p}{1-p} \Rightarrow z=\ln\!\left(\dfrac{p}{1-p}\right)$, valid for $p\in(0,1)$. Check: $\dfrac{p}{1-p}=\dfrac{e^z/(1+e^z)}{1/(1+e^z)}=e^z$. So logit $=$ logistic$^{-1}$, and $z$ = \ix{log odds}.\ixh{sigmoid}
\end{qbox}

\begin{qbox}{HW3 Q12 (231 only)}
\textbf{Prompt.} Assume that $z = \beta_0 + \beta_{1}x_{1}$ and $p = logistic(z)$. How do the \ix{odds} of the outcome change if you increase $x_{1}$ by two? Demonstrate this.

Assume now that $\beta_1$ is negative. What value does $p$ approach as $x_{1}$ approaches $\infty$? What value does $p$ approach as $x_{1}$ approaches $-\infty$? Demonstrate.
\ans Odds $=\dfrac{p}{1-p}=e^{z}=e^{\beta_0+\beta_1x_1}$ (Q11). Then
$\dfrac{\text{odds}(x_1+2)}{\text{odds}(x_1)}=\dfrac{e^{\beta_0+\beta_1(x_1+2)}}{e^{\beta_0+\beta_1x_1}}=e^{2\beta_1}$: the odds are \emph{multiplied} by $e^{2\beta_1}$ (\ix{odds ratio}; log odds increase by $2\beta_1$), regardless of $x_1$.
If $\beta_1<0$: $x_1\to\infty \Rightarrow z\to-\infty \Rightarrow e^z\to0 \Rightarrow p=\frac{e^z}{1+e^z}\to 0$. $x_1\to-\infty \Rightarrow z\to\infty \Rightarrow p=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\to \frac{1}{1+0}=1$.
\end{qbox}
