\slide{3}{61}{LDA: A motivating example}
\fig{2D \ix{scatterplot}: ``Gene X'' (horizontal) vs.\ ``Gene Y'' (vertical). 6 green points (drug works) lie mostly toward the lower-left; 6 red points (drug does not work) lie mostly along the top/upper-right, plus one red at the lower right. \begin{quote}Two genes do a better job separating the two categories.

Would three genes be even better?\end{quote}

\slide{3}{75}{[untitled --- distance between means alone vs means and scatter]}
\fig{Left: a 2D scatterplot: 6 red points in an upper band and 6 green points in a lower band, both bands rising gently to the right (separated vertically, each spread out horizontally).
Top right: the points are projected onto a line rising with the bands; the class means are far apart, but in the middle of the line green and red points are interleaved.
Bottom right: the points are projected onto a steep, nearly vertical line; the red points cluster tightly near the top and the green points tightly near the bottom, with no overlap.}\ixh{distance between means}\ixh{scatter}\ixh{projection}
\begin{itemize}
\item If we only maximize the distance between means\ldots\quad The separation isn't great\ldots
\item However, if we optimize the distance between means and scatter\ldots\quad We get nice separation!
\end{itemize}
